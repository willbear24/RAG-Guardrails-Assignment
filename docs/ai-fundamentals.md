# AI Fundamentals

Artificial intelligence describes computational methods for performing tasks that normally require human-like capabilities, including language understanding, perception, prediction, and decision support. Modern AI includes machine learning, where a model learns patterns from examples, and generative AI, where a model produces new content such as text, images, audio, or code. The word "intelligence" can obscure important differences: a system may produce fluent language without understanding it as a person does. Evaluate an AI system by its behavior on a defined task rather than by how confident or human its response sounds.

Most current large language models are neural networks trained to predict likely next tokens from preceding text. A token is a unit used by the model and may be a word, part of a word, or punctuation. Training adjusts model parameters to reduce prediction error across large datasets. At inference time, the model generates output using its learned parameters and the prompt. Sampling settings such as temperature can change output variability, but they do not make unsupported claims reliable. A model's training data and knowledge may be incomplete or out of date, and it may generate plausible-sounding errors known as hallucinations.

Prompting gives instructions and context for a model to use. Clear prompts define the task, relevant background, desired format, and constraints. Examples can demonstrate the expected pattern. However, a prompt cannot guarantee correctness, prevent every unsafe response, or substitute for validation. Treat model output as untrusted data: validate structured responses against a schema, escape text before rendering it in a browser or terminal, and never execute generated code or commands without appropriate review. Keep private information out of prompts unless the system is explicitly approved for that data.

Retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG, combines a language model with external information. A typical pipeline splits source documents into chunks, converts them to vector embeddings, stores the vectors with text and metadata, retrieves chunks similar to a question, and provides those chunks as context to the model. Chunk size and overlap affect whether relevant facts are kept together or split apart. Metadata can include source filenames and section names, allowing the system to show citations. Retrieval quality depends on the content, embedding model, query, distance metric, and indexing settings; a fluent answer does not prove that the correct source was retrieved.

Grounded generation asks the model to answer from retrieved context and acknowledge when that context is insufficient. Citations should point to source material actually provided to the model and support the associated claim. A system should not invent citations or present unrelated retrieved text as evidence. RAG can reduce reliance on model memory, but it does not remove hallucinations, prompt injection risks, stale documents, or retrieval errors. Retrieved documents themselves may contain adversarial instructions, so treat them as data rather than higher-priority instructions.

Evaluation should test the complete system, not only the model. Include questions that should be answerable from the documents, related questions with missing details, and unrelated questions. Assess whether retrieval finds relevant chunks, whether answers are supported, whether citations are accurate, and whether the system appropriately declines to guess. Monitor latency, failures, and changes in data quality. Consider privacy, licensing, bias, accessibility, and the consequences of mistakes. AI is most useful when its limits are understood, human responsibility is clear, and outputs can be checked against trustworthy evidence.
